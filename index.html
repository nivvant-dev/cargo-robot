02Project overview

Cargo movement, without closing the aisle.

A compact mobile robot picks stowed bags and boxes from a mock spacecraft module and hands them to a stowage shelf — keeping the central aisle open the whole time.

Inside a spacecraft, every item has a place, and moving it between those places costs crew time and attention. This prototype explores a small, low-cost way to take stowed cargo out of a rack and transfer it to a hand-off point while the aisle stays usable.

The robot drives down the marked centerline, identifies a bag or box, grips it gently, lifts it clear of the rack, carries it to the drop-off zone, and records the hand-off. We test on a flat-board mock module so the mechanism and sequence can be iterated quickly. A curved module shell is the planned later upgrade.

Prototype boundaries: soft cargo bags and light boxes up to about 1.5 kg, one item at a time, operator-supervised. Final requirements are confirmed against the project brief.

Success looks like

  • Lift and hold a 1 kg bag
  • Carry it 1 m without a drop
  • Stow it correctly in 8 of 10 trials
  • Finish a cycle in under 60 seconds
  • Keep the central aisle clear
  • Stop safely on command or fault

The aisle is a hard constraint

The robot and its lift must stay inside the marked lane while reaching cargo on either side. Cargo A–D positions, rack clearance and the hand-off shelf are all marked on the mock module before any powered testing.

04Concept & architecture

Two approaches, one build.

We started from a Cartesian reference design, then adapted it into a mobile prototype that could be built, tested and iterated with school resources.

A · Reference design

Cartesian rail + lift

A horizontal X rail carries a vertical Z lift with a gripper on the end. Simple kinematics and repeatable coordinates make it easy to reason about.

  • Structure: fixed V-slot frame, X gantry, Z column
  • Strength: rigid, precise, no base motion to stabilize
  • Trade-off: floor space and a large fixed envelope
B · Prototype concept Our build

VEX base + 3D-printed lift

A VEX drive base carries a 3D-printed vertical lift and gripper. The lift and gripper sit on the base, the vertical lift runs inside the mast, and the assembly can reach stacked cargo.

  • Structure: VEX chassis, printed mast, T8 lead screw, two-finger jaw
  • Strength: mobile, compact, quick to prototype and repair
  • Trade-off: mast stiffness, cable routing and tip-over margin

Build tip

Keep the lift jaw centered over the base to limit tipping moment, and test with lightweight boxes first. Add mass low on the chassis only after the unloaded motion path is proven.

05End effector

A grip that is strong enough to hold, gentle enough to trust.

A simple two-finger parallel gripper, printed in PETG, with compliant pads that create friction on soft cargo bags without crushing them.

Design & materials

Two parallel fingers close on the cargo under a single servo or VEX motor. Rubber foam or TPU pads sit on the finger faces to add friction and spread the grip load across the bag. An optional printed hook handles bags with loops or handles.

Grip force is deliberately modest: the bag should compress slightly, not deform permanently, and the finger tips must stay clear of the rack during approach.

  • No permanent deformation
  • No overheating

    08Build steps

    Eleven steps, each with a way to know it is done.

    The build order is deliberately conservative: get the frame rigid, prove motion by hand, then power one axis at a time. Every step ends with a verification check.

    Write down what a successful trial means, then mark the physical test envelope before any hardware goes in.

    • Set measurable pass/fail criteria for payload, repeatability, cycle time and safe stopping.
    • Mark the mock module envelope (≈1.2 × 0.6 × 0.6 m), aisle centerline and Cargo A–D positions.
    • Check school inventory against the BOM so orders only cover what is missing.

    ✓Verification

    Requirements sheet lists a number and test method for every criterion. Layout is taped and labelled. Inventory list matches the BOM, with gaps flagged for purchase.

    Build the test world first, so the robot has something stable to be measured against.

    • Assemble the flat-board mock module and secure the stowage bay so it cannot shift.
    • Weigh and label pouches at 0.5 kg, 1.0 kg and 1.5 kg, plus one box.
    • Print the jaws and mounts in PETG; start a project folder for CAD, wiring, firmware and test logs.

    ✓Verification

    The module does not move when bumped, each pouch mass is confirmed on a scale, and every printed part arrives at its mount with the intended clearance.

    A flexible base turns every belt tension and coordinate into a moving target. Build it stiff and square first.

    • Assemble the plywood base and extrusion frame; check diagonals for square.
    • Tighten brackets evenly and route later wiring away from wheels and pinch points.

    ✓Verification

    The frame is rigid, sits flat on the module floor with no rocking, and shows no visible flex when each corner is gently pushed.

    Set the rail straight and the carriage free before adding a belt or power.

    • Mount the V-slot rail straight and parallel to the aisle; set V-wheel tension without over-preloading.
    • Fit the GT2 belt, 20T pulley, idlers and clips; align the belt run with the carriage.
    • Install limit switches at both ends of travel, positioned to trip before the hard stops.
    • Move the carriage through its full stroke by hand.

    ✓Verification

    The carriage travels the full ~1.2 m with no binding or tight spots, belt tension is even on both runs, and each switch clicks before the carriage reaches its stop.

    The vertical axis carries the highest moment and the biggest consequence if it slips.

    • Brace the column in both directions; check it is plumb to the base.
    • Align the T8 lead screw, anti-backlash nut, coupler and top bearing so the screw turns freely by hand.
    • Fit upper and lower limit switches; add a mechanical restraint against unpowered drop.

    ✓Verification

    The lift moves smoothly by hand without screw bind, holds its position when unpowered, and both limit switches trip before the carriage bottoms out.

    Prove the jaw closes, holds and releases with the heaviest test load before it goes near a rack.

    • Bond or fasten the foam/TPU pads flat to both fingers; check they do not peel at the edges.
    • Set servo endpoints so the fingers close gently and stop before stalling.
    • Check finger clearance against the cargo and rack lip through the full approach.
    • Run the 30-second hold test from the End Effector section.

    ✓Verification

    The gripper holds the maximum test payload for 30 seconds with no slip, permanent bag deformation, overheating or servo stall — and releases cleanly on command.

    Wiring is done unpowered. The E-stop is a hardware cut, not a software suggestion.

    • Keep the supply unplugged while wiring; add a fuse holder and rocker switch on the incoming 12 V line.
    • Wire a normally-closed 22 mm E-stop so it cuts actuator power directly.
    • Give the servo rail its own 12→6 V 5 A buck converter; do not load servos from the controller regulator.
    • Check continuity, polarity and shorts with a multimeter before first power-up.
    • Set stepper driver current per its documentation; add heatsinks. Route cables through the drag chain.

    ✓Verification

    Continuity and polarity checks pass, the E-stop removes actuator power when pressed, the 6 V servo rail stays in spec under load, and no conductor or terminal is exposed.

    Bring up one motor at a time with conservative speeds, then calibrate against a ruler — not against the math alone.

    • Use AccelStepper for motion and the Servo library for the gripper.
    • Test each motor separately, unloaded or with the axis disconnected, before moving the mechanism.
    • Calibrate steps/mm and confirm by measuring a commanded move. Starting estimates: ≈80 steps/mm for 20T GT2 at 16× microstepping, ≈400 steps/mm for an 8 mm-lead screw.
    • Implement homing, then teleop with limit and soft-travel guards.
    • Validate the VL53L1X reading against a tape measure and set a usable range window.

    ✓Verification

    A commanded 100 mm move measures within tolerance on the ruler, homing repeats 10 times to the same position, teleop stops at every limit, and the ToF sensor agrees with a physical measurement inside its working window.

    Now the robot meets the rack, the shelf and the marked aisle. Power off for every clearance check.

    • With power off, walk the full travel envelope by hand and check clearances against the module, racks and shelf.
    • Record pick and stow coordinates for Cargo A–D and the drop-off zone.
    • Run the complete cycle manually before enabling any automatic sequence.

    ✓Verification

    Nothing contacts the module or rack through the full envelope, recorded coordinates repeat, and one manual full cycle completes start to hand-off without a fault.

    Encode the proven manual cycle as the state machine above, with a fault state that always ends in a safe stop.

    • Implement the sequence with explicit states and timeouts per move.
    • Add a fault state with a logged reason and operator reset; never auto-retry a fault.
    • Handle the lightest payload first, then step up mass only after a clean run.
    • Keep Start, Stop and E-stop behaviour unchanged from manual testing.

    ✓Verification

    Three full automatic cycles complete with the operator's hand on the E-stop, and every injected fault (timeout, limit, sensor, gripper) lands in a safe stop with a clear message.

    Run enough trials that the result is believable, and record the failures as carefully as the successes.

    • Run at least 10 trials per payload mass; log success, mass, cycle time, drops, collisions, faults and fixes in the interactive test log.
    • Capture photos and video of a complete cycle and of any failure worth explaining.
    • Finalize the BOM, wiring diagram, CAD, firmware version and test table for review.

    ✓Verification

    Every test row is filled with no blank results, the success rate and average cycle time are compared with the 8/10 and <60 s targets, and the final documentation set is complete and reproducible.

    10Safety

    Powered tests are supervised tests.

    The robot moves a rigid mast and a powered jaw in a small envelope. These rules apply to every powered session, not just the demo.

    01

    Test light payloads first

    Prove the motion path and gripper with an empty jaw and the lightest pouch before adding mass.

    02

    Hands clear of moving parts

    Keep fingers away from belts, pulleys, lead screws, carriages and jaws whenever power is on.

    03

    Never stand under a raised load

    Treat the lift as a suspended mass. No hands, heads or cables in the drop zone.

    04

    Verify the E-stop every session

    Press it once before testing and confirm it removes actuator power. Reset only after the fault is understood.

    05

    Clear the travel envelope

    Remove tools, bags and cables from the aisle and keep one supervisor at the controls for powered tests.

    06

    Stop on anything abnormal

    Overheating, a burnt smell, unusual noise or erratic motion means power off and diagnose — never keep cycling.

    ▲Safety glasses required for cutting, drilling and powered tests. Adult supervision required.
